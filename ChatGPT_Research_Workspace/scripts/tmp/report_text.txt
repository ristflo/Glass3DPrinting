Additive Manufacturing of Molten Glass
Evidence, Process Physics, Hardware, and an Engineering Roadmap
Glass 3D Printing Research Project
Technical evidence review and reproducible analysis
30 September 2026
Abstract
Molten-glass additive manufacturing connects glass chemistry, viscous flow, thermal his-
tory, motion planning, and defect-sensitive qualification. This review distinguishes reservoir
extrusion and laser filament deposition from powder-bed processing and indirect composite
routes. It synthesizes primary apparatus studies, manufacturer reference data, patent disclo-
sures, and commercial evidence while making gaps in measurement explicit. Reproducible
viscosity fits and flow/thermal scaling calculations explain process tradeoffs without present-
ing model scenarios as machine performance. A practical roadmap prioritizes feedstock iden-
tity, interlayer contact, temperature measurement, dimensional control, and end-use qualifi-
cation. The accompanying archive includes source records, downloaded literature, figures,
scripts, and the modular LaTeX project.
Evidence status. Initial sourcing occurred on 26 September 2026; targeted source verification con-
tinued on 29 September, with final editorial review on 30 September. This is a scoped technical
review, not a certified exhaustive systematic review or a design certification. Missing specifica-
tions are reported as unknown. Calculated scenarios are identified separately from experimental
findings.

1
Introduction, scope, and process taxonomy
1.1
The manufacturing question
The useful question is not simply whether glass can be printed. It is whether a specified composi-
tion can be formed into an accepted geometry, at a repeatable rate, with the required optical, me-
chanical, thermal, and chemical properties. A transparent sculpture, a microfluidic channel, and
a load-bearing glass connection place very different demands on the process. This report centers
on direct deposition of hot inorganic glass and uses other additive routes as explicitly identified
comparators.
The strongest case for additive manufacturing is geometric customization that is expensive or im-
practical with a repeated mold or accessible machining surface. Eliminating a mold does not elim-
inate conditioning, finishing, thermal treatment, or inspection. Conversely, accepting the native
layer texture can create useful optical and aesthetic functions. Decisions should therefore begin
with a part specification and a conventional-process baseline, not an assumption that additive
manufacture is preferable.
1.2
A taxonomy by material state and consolidation step
Reservoir-fed molten extrusion conditions a quantity of glass in a crucible and deposits it through
a heated nozzle. Gravity, imposed pressure, or mechanical metering drives flow. The receiving
chamber manages cooling while a motion system lays down a continuous cord. Early G3DP and
G3DP2 are reference demonstrations of this family.[18, 19]
Locally heated solid-feed deposition delivers a glass rod or filament to a hot zone. A furnace-
like nozzle or a laser heats only the material needed for deposition. Laser filament systems are
particularly relevant to fused silica, whose viscosity at the same temperature differs greatly from
soda-lime compositions. This route is analogous in material delivery to wire-fed directed-energy
deposition, but it requires glass-specific treatment of optical absorption and stress.[24]
Powder-bed fusion scans successive powder layers with a heat source. Packing, absorption, track
consolidation, residual porosity, and cracking become central variables. Laser scan speed is not
a net part-production rate: powder recoating, track overlap, contour passes, and rejected builds
must be included. The labels SLS and SLM in the glass literature should be interpreted from the
actual consolidation mechanism rather than treated as proof of complete densification.[7]
Indirect printing and conversion shapes silica-containing inks, photopolymer nanocomposites,
or precursors before drying, debinding, and densification. Stereolithography, two-photon poly-
merization, direct ink writing, and computed axial lithography belong here when the printed
object is a precursor rather than final dense glass. The printing temperature can be near room
temperature while subsequent firing is much hotter. Kotz and colleagues demonstrated transpar-
ent fused-silica structures by photopolymerizing a nanocomposite and heat treating it; Toombs
and colleagues demonstrated volumetric shaping followed by sintering.[20, 39]
These routes exclude glass-fiber-filled thermoplastics, sugar glass, and metallic glasses from the
core comparison. Chalcogenide glass extrusion is considered in the academic directory as a dis-
tinct material family, not as evidence for the same silicate processing window. Additively manu-
facturing a metal mold used to form glass is also an adjacent tooling route rather than direct glass
printing.

Rheology, thermal properties, and material physics
2.1
A glass name is not a constitutive law
Composition, hydroxyl content, impurities, and thermal history influence the response of glass.
“Borosilicate” and “soda-lime” identify families rather than unique viscosity curves. Fused quartz
and synthetic fused silica are also not automatically interchangeable grades. Accordingly, the
calculations below use three named manufacturer products as reference materials: SCHOTT AR-
GLAS, SCHOTT BOROFLOAT 33, and Corning HPFS 7980. They are not asserted to be the feed-
stocks used by the cited printers.[6, 35, 36]

2.4
Instability, slumping, and interlayer contact
For an ideal inviscid free cylindrical jet in a dynamically negligible exterior fluid, the fastest grow-
ing axisymmetric capillary mode has wavelength approximately 9.02r = 4.51d. That familiar
8


Rayleigh result is not a universal optimum for a viscous glass bead contacting a substrate. Vis-
cosity contrast, cooling, stretching, finite length, contact angle, and the receiving surface change
the instability problem; Tomotika’s viscous-thread treatment illustrates the importance of the sur-
rounding fluid.[33, 38]
The viscous capillary scale tγ ∼ηr/γ is about 16.7 s for the preceding scenario. A gravitational
deformation scale tg ∼η/(ρgH) is about 0.41 s for an assumed unsupported height H = 0.1
m. These order-of-magnitude times refer to a material that remains at the selected viscosity. A
rapidly cooling, attached bead violates that assumption, and neither value is an observed failure
time. They show why shape arrest and bonding must be controlled together.
Surface-tension gradients can impose tangential stresses, η ∂ut/∂n ∼∂γ/∂s. Temperature and
composition variations can therefore drive Marangoni flow even when bulk inertia is small. Quan-
tifying it requires the actual derivative of surface tension and local thermal field; a generic sign or
magnitude is not assigned here. Similarly, capillary neck growth between adjacent beads is only
one contribution to adhesion. Contact area, surface contamination, and the time-temperature his-
tory must accompany any comparison of interlayer strength.
2.5
Thermal scales and materials outside the fitted set
Using BOROFLOAT’s reference k = 1.2 W/(m K) at 90 ◦C, cp = 830 J/(kg K) over 20–100 ◦C,
and nominal density 2230 kg/m3 gives an approximate low-temperature diffusivity a ≈6.48 ×
10−7 m2/s. Characteristic diffusion times L2/a are approximately 39 s at L = 5 mm and 617 s
at 20 mm. These are scaling calculations using low-temperature reference properties, not high-
temperature annealing schedules. Radiation, temperature-dependent properties, and boundary
conditions become significant in a real print.[36]
Aluminosilicates add a broad composition space with varying modifiers, liquidus behavior, and
viscosity. No single aluminosilicate curve is defensibly inferred from the three products above. A
useful extension would specify a grade and obtain measured viscosity, expansion, crystallization
kinetics, and compatible wet-contact materials before fitting. This explicit gap is preferable to
assigning plausible-looking coeﬀicients to an unspecified composition.
3
Nozzle mechanics, hardware, and kinematics
3.1
Two materially different ways to deliver hot glass
Reservoir extrusion and laser-heated filament deposition solve different hardware problems. In
the former, a crucible provides an inventory of conditioned melt and a nozzle meters a continuous
cord. In the latter, a solid feedstock carries material to a small hot zone; deposition occurs after
local softening or melting. Separating these architectures prevents an inappropriate comparison
between a furnace setpoint and a laser’s nominal power. Both are direct hot-glass routes, but
their thermal inventory, material residence time, attainable feature size, and interruption behavior
differ.
The historical MIT G3DP apparatus demonstrated transparent reservoir-fed material extrusion
with a heated receiving chamber. Its institutional project description specifies an upper kiln car-
tridge and a lower annealing chamber. The later G3DP2 paper describes three-zone thermal con-
trol and four-axis motion, alongside a demonstration of assembled architectural columns. These
publications establish feasibility and increasingly integrated process control; they do not establish
that every printed component is suitable for structural service without qualification.[18, 19, 28]

3.3
Motion control is also material control
Ignoring shrinkage and evaporation, conservation of mass relates deposited area Ab to travel speed
U through Ab ≃Q/U. A corner slowdown therefore increases local cross-section unless flow
changes at the same time. Conversely, acceleration can thin a bead and reduce contact with the
previous layer. Discontinuous paths add a second problem: molten glass stored downstream
of a metering mechanism can continue moving after its command stops. Designing a path as a
continuous loop reduces repeated starts, although it restricts accessible topology.
These observations motivate a proposed controller with separate estimation of thermal state, ma-
terial delivery, and position. A geometric planner should specify the desired cross-section and
contact location; a deposition controller should translate these into speed and flow commands
with actuator delays. The controller should monitor actual bead shape rather than interpreting
successful motor motion as successful deposition. Height feedback is particularly valuable be-
cause small layer errors accumulate and change the physical gap between nozzle or hot zone and
the growing part.
Laser filament systems remove the need for a large nozzle filled with molten silica. Von Witzen-
dorff and colleagues demonstrated quartz-fiber deposition using a CO2 laser and translating sub-
strate. Liu and colleagues subsequently examined symmetric laser heating and bonding of ap-
proximately 196 µm fused-silica filament; their multilayer experiment used 200 µm nominal layers.
10


These are research operating conditions, not a universal printer resolution.[24, 43] Their signifi-
cance is that energy delivery, filament feed, and substrate motion jointly determine fusion. Nomi-
nal laser power alone cannot specify the delivered energy density without optical coupling, beam
geometry, feedstock diameter, and speed.

A useful acceptance sequence begins with stationary thermal characterization, proceeds to single

path geometry is an incomplete description of usable manufacturing capacity.
4
In-situ thermal management and annealing
4.1
Deposition temperature and annealing temperature serve different purposes
Hot-glass printing must establish intimate contact at a new interface while preserving the geome-
try already deposited. Heating promotes flow and bonding but also allows deformation; cooling
arrests geometry but develops gradients and eventually freezes stress. A heated build chamber
consequently addresses only part of the problem. It does not automatically certify completion of
annealing, and a chamber thermocouple does not directly measure the coldest or hottest point
inside a thick section.
G3DP2’s separated thermal zones demonstrate an architecture that can manage melt prepara-
tion, delivery, and the receiving environment independently.[18] At smaller scales, Chen and col-
leagues report a closed-loop system adjusting CO2 laser power from measured melt-zone temper-
ature. Their quartz-glass experiments compare temperature-controlled deposition with constant-
11


power cases and report improved formation quality. The paper supports feedback as an experi-
mentally tested route; it does not establish that its sensor calibration, controller gains, or power
limits transfer to another glass or optical arrangement. The article appeared online in December
2024 in the 2025 journal issue.[3]

4.3
Stress relaxation and cooling history
SCHOTT’s technical guidance identifies annealing conditions, glass type, size, and geometry as
determinants of residual stress. Its conventional upper annealing and strain-point viscosities are
1013 and 1014.5 dPa s, respectively, equivalent to 1012 and 1013.5 Pa s. Confusing these units shifts
a viscosity curve by one decade. Fine annealing involves temperature equalization and controlled
cooling through the relevant relaxation range; rapid cooling farther below that range can still cause
damaging temporary stress.[34]
For interpretation, a Maxwell estimate gives a stress-relaxation time τM ∼η/G, where G is an
appropriate shear modulus. The ratio τM/tprocess compares relaxation with the imposed thermal
history. It is not a universal annealing recipe: real glass exhibits a relaxation spectrum and struc-
tural history dependence. Likewise, the restrained elastic estimate

4.4
A proposed measurement-driven annealing workflow
A defensible qualification program would first measure or obtain the specific batch’s transition,
expansion, and relaxation characteristics. Next, instrument a representative thick-section surro-
gate to estimate how chamber readings relate to internal temperature. Use that information to
propose a dwell and cooling program, and verify the resulting parts by calibrated stress measure-
ment and dimensional inspection. A hollow wall, solid base, and junction may require different
local interpretation even within one object.
For thermal imaging, record spectral band, viewing geometry, reflected surroundings, assumed
emissivity, and calibration method. A visually stable thermal image is not evidence of an accurate
absolute temperature if the camera sees transmitted or reflected radiation. Recycled-glass printing
work by Stern and colleagues explicitly investigated glass-dependent viscosity and used infrared
thermography during extrusion, emphasizing why changing feedstock requires renewed process
characterization.[37]
In-situ heating and a separate annealing furnace should therefore be evaluated as a complete pro-
duction schedule. Include transfer delays, loading orientation, furnace occupancy, and the slowest
qualified cooling step when estimating throughput. A continuous lehr is a possible industrial de-
sign, but its residence-time distribution and compatibility with individually printed geometries
would need demonstration. No operating continuous annealing lehr is inferred from the mere
presence of a heated build chamber in the cited prototypes.

Defect mechanisms and optical quality
5.1
A transparent object is not necessarily an imaging optic
Optical qualification should separate total transmission, directional transmission, wavefront qual-
ity, and scattering. A ribbed transparent wall can transmit considerable light while producing
strong image distortion. Conversely, a deliberately patterned illumination element may use that
distortion productively. The early G3DP work demonstrates transparent structures and control-
lable caustic patterns; it is not evidence that an as-printed wall has the surface figure of a polished
lens.[19]
Destino and colleagues provide a different benchmark: silica and silica–titania glasses prepared
by sol–gel-derived ink writing followed by thermal processing were characterized against com-
mercial optical glasses. Their work includes polished surfaces and refractive-index homogeneity,
so those results should not be attributed to as-deposited molten cords.[8] Cross-route compar-
isons need the whole process history, including polishing, thermal densification, coatings, and
measurement path length.
5.2
Mechanism-based diagnosis
Entrained gas, inter-track voids, crystalline inclusions, foreign particles, and surface damage can
all reduce performance but demand different remedies. Gas already in a reservoir calls for feed-
stock and conditioning investigation. Voids recurring at junctions suggest deposition geometry or
insuﬀicient bonding. A rough surface can arise from layer shape without implying bulk porosity.
Crystals require phase-sensitive characterization; their presence must not be inferred from every
bright spot in a microscope image.
Devitrification depends on composition, nucleation sites, temperature, and exposure time. A liq-
uidus temperature describes an equilibrium phase boundary, not a complete kinetic guarantee
that a print remains amorphous. Recommended characterization includes differential scanning
calorimetry at stated heating rates, diffraction, and microscopy after representative thermal cy-
13


cles. Recycling further demands chemical identity and contamination checks. Mixing individually
transparent cullets does not establish thermal compatibility or a common viscosity curve. These
are proposed qualification measures, not claims that the cited recycled-glass study demonstrated
every test.
For optical loss, a basic homogeneous attenuation model is
Tint(λ, d) = exp[−µ(λ)d].
(10)
External transmission also includes interface reflections, collection geometry, and scattering. For a
simple plane-parallel transparent specimen, surface reflection losses cannot be silently assigned to
bulk absorption. Reporting a single percentage without wavelength, thickness, surface condition,
and whether scattered light is collected is insuﬀicient for ranking processes. Likewise, roughness
Ra is a profile statistic at a stated cutoff and direction; it does not alone specify optical wavefront
quality.
5.3
Interlayer bonding and mechanical evidence
The KTH filament study measured both line geometry and bonding width, providing evidence
that deposited width and actual contact width need not be equal. Its multilayer results demon-
strate reproducible bonding under the selected operating conditions, not an independently mea-
sured universal tensile strength.[24] A visible line in a transparent print may represent surface
geometry, an internal interface, compositional variation, or stress; fracture analysis is needed to
identify the feature that actually controls failure.
A 2026 study by Chhadeh and colleagues directly addresses mechanical performance of extruded
soda-lime glass. Its four-point bending experiments show dependence on toolpath and layer ori-
entation and connect performance to thermal retention during deposition. The reported 90◦spec-
imens had mean strength approximately 40% greater than the 0◦specimens in that experiment.
This is a within-study comparison, not a transferable design factor. The test setup deliberately dif-
fers from EN 1288-3 in dimensions and roller freedom; it should not be described as unqualified
standard conformity.[4]
The engineering implication is that orientation labels alone are inadequate predictors: they can
also change path length, return time, and the heat history of adjacent tracks. A proposed experi-
mental design should hold those factors separately where possible and record specimen surface
treatment, effective loaded area, loading rate, environment, and failure origin. Report the spec-
imen count and full strength distribution rather than the strongest coupon. Comparing flexural
strength with direct tensile strength, or comparing specimens with different flaw populations, can
obscure the actual process improvement.

6.1
How to interpret the research network
The most useful collaboration depends on the missing capability. Reservoir extrusion needs melt
conditioning, refractory compatibility, motion coordination, and annealing capacity. Fused-silica
filament deposition depends more directly on laser delivery, feedstock uniformity, and local bond-
ing. Optical-quality ink routes instead place substantial demands on colloid formulation, drying,
organic removal, densification, and finishing. These categories overlap in characterization but do
not share interchangeable processing temperatures or material supply chains.
Two attribution errors deserve explicit correction. First, publication in proceedings hosted at TU
Delft does not mean that the printer belongs to TU Delft: the 2024 recycled-glass extrusion study
identifies Evenline and MIT authors.[37] Second, the KTH article’s aﬀiliations identify KTH and
Nobula; its editor’s RISE aﬀiliation is not evidence that RISE operated that apparatus. The separate
institutional collaboration record establishes RISE involvement at project level, with a narrower
evidentiary claim.[22, 24]
Laval is a verified direct-glass-extrusion contributor, but its arsenic-sulfide composition belongs to
the chalcogenide family and uses a substantially different processing window from silicates.[1] Its
later nanocomposite-resin project is an additional route, not evidence that the chalcogenide plat-
form prints silica.[41] McGill and Nagaoka remain explicit search gaps for the requested direct
molten inorganic-glass directory. Resolving them would require a primary apparatus paper, the-
sis, patent, or laboratory record identifying both the material and the deposition process; general
expertise in glass or additive manufacturing is insuﬀicient.
This evidence structure favors reproducible comparisons and appropriate research partnerships.
A prospective collaboration brief should specify composition, size range, required optical function,
and final thermal treatment before naming a preferred institution. Such a brief is more informative
than a broad list of organizations associated with the phrase “glass 3D printing.”

7.3
Application fit and competitive alternatives
The strongest near-term case for large molten beads is custom shape and visible material expres-
sion. Art, lighting, and limited-run packaging concepts can accept a layered surface when it is part
of the design. Functional packaging adds closure tolerances, cleanability, impact resistance, and
repeatability; an attractive vessel alone does not establish suitability for food, pharmaceuticals, or
pressurization. Structural masonry is supported by specimen and assembly research, while broad
architectural deployment remains an inference requiring application testing [26].
For lenses, optical channels, and chemical microdevices, high value per part can justify slower
fabrication and metrology. Laser filament deposition has direct micro-optical evidence, and com-
posite routes have supplier offerings in optics and microfluidics [15, 25]. Nevertheless, hermetic
chemical vessels require verified leak tightness, pressure capability, connection integrity, and
chemical compatibility for the actual fluid and temperature. Radioactive-waste vitrification con-
tainers are treated here as a proposed research direction: this source set establishes neither a qual-
ified printed containment product nor a commercial deployment. Glass chemistry and a printable
shape are insuﬀicient evidence of long-term containment performance.
Conventional alternatives remain the baseline for purchasing decisions. Blowing and lampwork-
ing offer skilled manipulation of hollow forms; casting spreads tooling and furnace costs over
repeated parts; machining and polishing support controlled surfaces on suitable blanks. Indi-
rect AM introduces binder removal, shrinkage compensation, and sintering, whereas molten de-

position transfers diﬀiculty into thermal control, bead geometry, and annealing. These process
differences motivate a matched-part comparison: identical composition or function, dimensional
tolerances, inspected surface area, accepted yield, and annual volume. Comparing a millimetre
optical component with a kilogram decorative print on cost per kilogram would conceal the prod-
uct value and the inspection burden.

8.2
Measurements that could change engineering decisions
Closed-loop deposition. The controller should distinguish sensor validity from control perfor-
mance. Glass transparency, emissivity variation, hot background reflections, and laser interfer-
ence can bias a thermal reading. Validate a sensing arrangement before tuning control gains,
and evaluate unseen geometries rather than only training trajectories. Published quartz-glass
temperature feedback provides a concrete starting point, with apparatus-specific calibration still
necessary.[3]
Toolpath and thermal memory. Direction, speed, and return time are coupled. The 2026 tool-
path study supports testing orientation and retained heat together, while also illustrating why
a within-study strength improvement should not be converted directly into a structural design
factor. A factorial or response-surface programme should record the physical path and interface
temperature rather than just a slicer orientation label.[4]
Recycled feedstock. Sorting and conditioning are upstream process controls. A comparison
should separate a known single-source cullet stream from mixed unknown glass. Quantify batch
variation, contamination introduced during grinding or handling, and the fraction rejected before
printing. Recycled-glass demonstrators establish feasibility; closed-loop recycling and environ-
mental benefit require additional mass and energy accounting.[37]
Optical and structural function. Freeform illumination, micro-optics, chemical channels, and
masonry connections demand different validation. High transmission does not establish a low
wavefront error; compression testing of a brick does not establish impact resistance or connection
22


durability. Application-specific acceptance is the mechanism by which a research demonstrator
becomes a credible product.

context. The asset ledger retains direct streaming links where established. An HTTP response
confirms accessibility at the request time, not successful playback or the authenticity of every
quantitative claim in a video. The supplied media notes identify provenance and reproduction
terms separately from scientific interpretation.
8.4
Reproducibility and remaining evidence gaps
Direct demonstration links retained in the asset ledger are MIT Glass I / G3DP, MIT Glass II /
G3DP2, and Inside Maple Glass Printing. The University of Memphis polariscope page links a
direct educational demonstration. These links were checked for HTTP accessibility; playback was
not verified. The polariscope video is not claimed to show a printed-glass specimen.
The analysis scripts regenerate nominal viscosity fits, residual plots, flow sensitivity, thermal
scales, and the commercial scenarios. The viscosity thresholds CSV records extrapolation flags.
All calculations use explicit inputs and are distinct from experimentally measured machine out-
put. The complete LaTeX project permits updating a figure, source, or chapter without replacing
the underlying evidence trail.
Important gaps remain: matched-route tensile datasets; wavelength-, thickness-, and surface-
controlled optical comparisons; independently audited current printer prices and yields; material-
specific refractory lifetime; and verified direct molten-silicate apparatus attribution for some re-
quested institutions. These are limits of the evidence base, not blanks filled with estimates. Future
updates should add new primary measurements and revise the corresponding decision, rather
than merely extend a list of references.
The practical recommendation is to choose a material route from the required final function. Use
reservoir extrusion where large continuous forms and layer texture are compatible with the prod-
uct; investigate laser filament deposition where local heating and small features are valuable; eval-
uate indirect routes where fine geometry and densification can be qualified. In each case, measure
the complete path to an accepted part, including finishing and thermal treatment.

To date, there has been a paucity of results regarding mechanical properties of additively
manufactured (AM) glass. The ﬁndings herein provide valuable insights into optimising AM processes
for glass, aiming to enhance the reliability, structural performance, and practical application of glass
components in both engineering and architectural contexts. This study investigates the mechanical
behaviour of glass components fabricated using a Fused Deposition Modelling (FDM) type technique.
The research focuses on understanding the bending strength and overall structural integrity of FDM-
manufactured glass, in comparison to conventionally prepared glass, with particular attention to the
layer-to-layer bonding imparted by the AM process. Soda-lime glass rods, produced via an automated
pulling method, were used as ﬁlament feedstock for the AM process. Mechanical testing, including
four-point bending, highlighted the inﬂuence of layer orientation and AM toolpath on mechanical
performance. The results show toolpath optimisation can improve strength by controlling heat
retention during deposition.
Glass has long been a material of choice in numerous engineering appli-
cations due to its transparency, durability, and resistance to environmental
factors. Traditional glass manufacturing methods, such as ﬂoat glass pro-
duction and casting, have been extensively studied and optimised. The ﬂoat
process is the standard industrial process for ﬂat glass production for more
than60years.Thematerialpropertiesofglass,suchasstrength,aretherefore
known and accepted, including in standardisation (see EN 572-11). How-
ever, these methods often face limitations when it comes to producing
complex shapes. The recent advent of additive manufacturing (AM) tech-
niques offers a potential solution to these challenges, allowing for greater
design freedom and the ability to tailor mechanical properties.
The direct AM (i.e., ‘3D printing’) of glass presents signiﬁcant chal-
lenges due to the high melting temperatures required, the non- linear
thermomechanical properties, the development of undesired residual stress
during the cool process, and the inherent brittleness of the material.
Additionally, the low process tolerance and the necessity for achieving full
density further complicate the AM process. Even if the ﬁeld of AM glass is
difﬁcult and new, various AM glass techniques have become established in
recent years. The four most common are brieﬂy explained here. Selective
laser melting (SLM) uses a high-power laser to fuse glass powder layer by
layer, resulting in precise and intricate, but also opaque structures2,3. Fused
deposition modelling (FDM), also known as Fused ﬁlament facrication
(FFF), involves extruding molten glass through a nozzle, which solidiﬁes
upon deposition, making it suitable for larger forms, as depicted in Fig. 14–6.
Stereolithography (SLA) uses a laser to cure a photosensitive resin mixed
with glass particles, creating high-resolution and complex shapes7–9. Lastly,
Directed energy deposition (DED) involves feeding glass in the form of
powder or wire into a focused energy source, such as a laser or electron
beam, allowing for the creation of large and robust components10–13.
Speciﬁcally in regards to the present study, FDM, a process that
involves the layer-by-layer deposition of a semi-solid material to create a
three-dimensional object, is still a novel process with the material glass. This
method provides several advantages, including precise control over the
geometry and the ability to fabricate intricate structures. Despite these
advantages, the mechanical performance of FDM-fabricated glass compo-
nents is not known, particularly concerning the integrity of layer-to-layer
connections. The reasons for concern include an inhomogeneous joining
zone and sharp edge between the individual layers as well as the resulting
residual stress at uneven process temperatures.
The objective of the present study is to characterise the mechanical
behaviour of AM glass components produced via the FDM process.
Understanding the mechanical properties of these components is crucial for
their potential application in engineering and architectural contexts, where
reliability and structural integrity are paramount. To achieve this, a series of

The advancement of AM technologies has potential to revolutionise
glass fabrication - ﬁnally enabling reproducible, intricate and customised
glass components with high precision (and without the need for human
intervention or artisanal skills). This may be realised by either standalone
(ﬁnal net-shape) AM glass components or AM glass components fused to
either existing glass products or other AM prepared components.
AM glass has emerged as a tool to simplify material manipulation
throughtheuseofdigitaldesignsandautomation.Theabilitytoexpeditethe
feedbackloopwith glassmaterials,fromconcepttocreation,holdsimmense
potential for various industries. The industries in which AM glass is cur-
rently active include art and design, architecture, education, research and
optics.Ineachoftheseindustries,traditionalglassproductionrequiresyears
of glass working experience or outsourcing to factories. AM glass offers the
capability to rapidly manufacture glass components onsite. This is parti-
cularly beneﬁcial to scenarios where delivery is not feasible (e.g., space or
submarine environments) or to reduce reliance on supply chains.
In the case of art and design, AM glass enables the creation of unique
geometries not possible with traditional glass manufacturing techniques.
Internalstructures,assemblies, thinfeatures and interlockingparts are more
easily created without the need for skilled artistry (e.g., glassblowing or
lampworking) or additional tooling costs (e.g., moulds). This allows for
bespoke designs in interior design and commercial use, including storage
containers, lighting pieces, furniture and jewellery. See for example Fig. 1.
For research and development, this study exempliﬁed how AM glass
enabled mechanical testing to commence quickly, without the need for a
mould or intensive manual labour. In optical applications, there is ongoing
interest in using AM glass for optical ﬁbre and lens applications. Pan14
investigated the AM of glass preforms and successfully drew them into
optical ﬁbres, indicating a promising future for complex microstructured
ﬁbre fabrication.
AM glass also offers the ability to exploit the use of ﬁbres during the
printing process. The precise control of ﬁbrous glass structures holds
excitingpotential.Additionally,manyAM glasstechniquesallow the userto
pause production, enabling modiﬁcation during the print, such as embed-
ding components or introducing unique materials.
In addition to the potential for the production of three-dimensional
objects from glass, the possibility of fusing glass onto existing glass products
presents a vast array of applications.
In the present era, designers are investigating the potential of AM. A
multitudeofconceptscanbeactualisedthroughtheuseofglassasamaterial.
Existent glass products can also be modiﬁed through the application of 3D
printing technology. Flat objects are particularly amenable to this process.
Printing can also be employed on glass tubes or other shapes.
The potential for printing on ﬂat glass panes offers a means to achieve
both enhanced design ﬂexibility and load bearing capacity of the glass, see
ref. 15. In ref. 16, an analytical study was conducted for the construction
industry with the objective of optimising the deﬂection of a glass façade
under load by applying stiffeners. The results of this study demonstrate that
the application of stiffeners can result in material savings of up to 70% for a
two sided supported glass plate. Additionally, the study highlights that
signiﬁcantsavingscanbeachievedevenwhentheplacementofthestiffeners
is not optimal. This provides designers with considerable design freedom,
allowing for innovative solutions that may not have been previously con-
sidered. For instance, the redirection of solar radiation and sound waves
could be utilised to enhance the comfort of the interior environment.
The conceivable applications in the ﬁeld of architecture can be con-
tinued indeﬁnitely, AM glass can be used to apply stiffeners but also fused
ﬁxings for ﬂat glass, see ref. 17. It would also be possible to enhance the
production of insulating glazing units by permanently sealing the units by
using AM glass and additionally create the spacers for vacuum insulating
glazing units.

Specimen production
A dog-bone geometry was selected as the specimen type. The 3D model was
ﬁrst created using computer aided design software. This was then inputted
into the slicing software to prepare the ﬁles for 3D printing. All samples
featured zero perimeters and 100% rectilinear inﬁll, with various inﬁll
orientations. ‘PrusaSlicer 2.6’ was employed to prepare the 45° and 90°
samples. However, the 0° samples were created using ‘Grasshopper 3D +
Rhino’, to optimise the tool path. This is because the tool path generated by
the slicing software was inefﬁcient, with potential to create inconsistent
results. Settings remained consistent between all samples, to ensure com-
parable parameters.
A challenge encountered during the dog-bone production involved
achieving consistent levelling of the ﬁrst layer. Manual mesh bed levelling
was employed, involving the probing of six points, which were inspected by
the operator before each print. Human error is likely introduced during this
process, as observing deviations smaller than 1mm through the viewing
window of the heated chamber is challenging. This resulted in different
levels of adhesion to the printing platform for eachsample.In some samples
curlingofthecornerswasobserved.Thiseffectworsened iftheprintingpath
created a shearing force on these low adhesion areas.
Efforts were undertaken to maintain consistent parameters during the
production of specimens, with the exception of toolpaths, which varied
signiﬁcantly across series to achieve different layer orientations. These
variations in toolpaths, particularly regarding thermal management, had a
substantial impact on the manufactured specimens.
Theorientationof0°speciﬁesthatthelayersareorientedinthedirection
ofbending.Thevalueof90°indicatesthatthelayersareorientedinadirection
transverse to the direction of bending. Finally, the value of 45° represents the
orientation of the layers at an angle of 45° to the direction of bending.
Accordingly, the sample code follows the format ‘inﬁll angle – sample
number’. A representation of the layer progressions can be found in Fig. 3.
The toolpaths are detailed as follows:
For the 90° series, the paths were short and rapidly repeated, allowing
previously printed layers to retain heat. Additionally, nozzle radiation
continuously warmed the contact surfaces. As an example, Fig. 4 shows a
dog-bone specimen with the 90° inﬁll pattern. In the 45° series, the paths
were approximately 50 percent longer than those of the 90° series. The
nozzle paused twice during the printing of the web to complete external
corners, moving more slowly at the corners of layer lines compared to the
90° series. The 0° series involved extremely long paths, with the nozzle
printing over distances exceeding 300 mm to reach certain contact points.
Outer contours were printed ﬁrst, resulting in the subsequently printed
internal lines encountering cooler contact points.

Evaluation and Comparison
The experimental results, compared in Fig. 8, show a clear dependence on
the print direction. A correlation analysis (Spearman) between the layer
orientationangles(0°/45°/90°)and thefracturestressindicatesthatthereisa
statistically signiﬁcant (p-value = 0.03) and a large correlation (correlation
value of 0.56) according to Cohen21. The strong scattering of the results of
the 45° specimens compared to the other directions reduces the correlation
coefﬁcient substantially.
It was initially hypothesised that the 90° layer orientation would pro-
mote premature crack propagation, due to the critical notch orientation.
However, contrary to expectations, the 90° specimens exhibited the highest
fracture stress (mean value 56 N/mm2), while the 0° specimens showed the
lowest (mean value 40 N/mm2). Therefore, the orientation of the layers
should be taken into account in the design of the components, in combi-
nation with the printing strategy.
One probable cause for this phenomenon is the tool path itself. In the
specimens with a 90° orientation, the glass remains in a warmerstate, which
facilitates its effective fusion with the newly deposited glass. During the long
distances the nozzle travels while producing the 0° specimens the cooling
process of deposited glass impacts the layer-to-layer adhesion, leading to
reduced fracture stress.

correlation was identiﬁed between the number and size of these voids and
the fracture stress of the specimen. The highest-performing specimens (90°
series) exhibited a paucity of voids (see Fig. 9a), whereas specimens from the
45° (see Fig. 9b) and 0° (see Fig. 9c) series displayed a considerable pre-
valence of such voids. It is noteworthy that the strongest specimen of the 45°
series exhibited a low number of voids, which aligns with this observation.
These observations were made solely on the fracture surface. To provide
robust support for this hypothesis, further sectional analysis of the speci-
mens, both longitudinally and transversely, would be necessary.
Figure 8 also shows that the rods have a higher average fracture stress
than the fabricated dog-bone specimens. The decrease in this fracture stress
(which for brittle materials determines the material’s strength and is here-
after referred to as ’strength’) with an increase in surface under tension can
be described by the size effect of structural strength. The size effect on
structural strength is a phenomenon observed in materials, particularly
brittle materials such as glass, where the strength of a structure decreases as
its size increases22,23. This effect is primarily due to the increased probability
of encountering critical ﬂaws or imperfections within the material as its
surface area grows. The larger the structure, the more likely it is that a ﬂaw
will exist that can precipitate failure under stress.

mechanical behaviour of AM soda-lime glass components. The 90°

specimens exhibited a mean strength value approximately 40% higher than
the 0° specimens. Additionally, the glass ﬁlament samples showed a 30%
increase in mean strength value compared to the 90° specimens. The initial
hypothesis proposed that the 0° specimens would withstand the greatest
loading force before failure, with the 90° specimens expected to fail ﬁrst.
However, the experimental results showed the opposite. This hypothesis
was based on fracture mechanics theories, which suggest that glass typically
fractures at defects or discontinuities24. The layers in an AM component can
be considered discontinuities, implying that failure is likely to occur at the
interfaces between printed layers. In the 90° specimens, the inﬁll direction
forms discontinuities aligned orthogonal to the longitudinal axis, theoreti-
cally providing a low-energy fracture path. However, the disparity between
theory and experimental results can be attributed to the toolpath required
for each inﬁll orientation. The 90° conﬁguration uses a shorter, more direct
toolpath with minimal travel movements, which promotes localisedheating
and maintainshighertemperatureswithinthe recentlydepositedregions.In
contrast, the 0° orientation requires extended travel moves, leading to less
effective heat accumulation and greater thermal dissipation. Moreover, the
longer deposition paths in the 0° samples cause the tool head to alternate
between recentlydeposited and cooler regions,potentially resulting in lower
interlayer bonding strength compared to the 90° conﬁguration. This sug-
gests that toolpath continuity may directly inﬂuence local heat retention,
whichinturnaffectsthedegreeoffusionbetweenlayers.Fracturemechanics
theories align with experimental results when considering the crack origin.
In almost all 90° test specimens, see Fig. 5, the crack origin was commonly
observed at the layer discontinuities, on the tension side. For 0° and 45°
specimens, it is difﬁcult to make conclusive statements regarding crack
origin locations. Given that the fracture lines in the 90° specimens were not
observed to follow the discontinuity lines, this indicates that strong inter-
layer bonds were successfully formed during production.
TheseﬁndingsindicatethatAMglassspecimensproducedviaﬁlament
extrusion exhibit a complex relationship between the thermal proﬁles
generated by the AM toolpath and interlayer bond strength. A critical
insight from this investigation is that the toolpath signiﬁcantly affects
heating,therebyinﬂuencinginterlayerbondingstrength.Itisrecommended
that continuous and concise deposition paths be employed during printing
to optimise the thermal effects from nozzle-generated radiation and con-
vection. Furthermore, the coefﬁcient of variation for all 3D-printed
componentsaveraged 0.16, signiﬁcantlylower than the industry standard of
0.3 for sheet glass20. This could be an indication of the consistency and
reliability of the glass AM process, particularly if a consistent toolpath
(ensuring consistent heat distribution) is employed.
Future advancements in this research could explore advanced tool-
paths involving non-planar movements. The results show the dependence
of structural performance on the toolpath. These aspects must be further
investigated to reﬁne printing processes and to determine the optimum
deposition strategy for layering, tailored to the speciﬁc requirementsof each
application. In the immediate term, investigations into parameters such as
printing speed, layer count (part thickness), deposition size, and printing
temperatures could provide valuable insights. Speciﬁcally, a detailed
investigation into the formation of pores within the AM specimens should
also be conducted. Increasing the overlap between layers to reduce pore size
would be of interest in assessing how this inﬂuences fracture strength.
Future work should increase the sample size of AM test specimens to
enhance statistical analysis and further investigate variation between com-
ponents. Additionally, post-processing techniques, including reheating or
chemical and thermal pre-stressing, hold promise for enhancing the
material properties, like strength. With this knowledge of the process and
the material, an understanding of the structural behaviour of complex
geometries,such asthoseshowninFig. 1,canbeestimatedinthefuture.The
results of this paper are an important step forward in ensuring that the
visions outlined in Chapter 2 will be realised. The ability to pause AM
processes offers opportunities to integrate powders, alternative materials, or
objects, thereby increasing versatility. The evolution of glass AM is expected
to beneﬁt from a range of hardware and software innovations. Notable
directions include adjustments to nozzle outlet shape and size and the
introduction of composite materials through co-extrusion.

to commonly fabricate glass ﬁlament (i.e., rods), for glass 3D printing. An
advantage of using the Vitri-Glass system is the ability to select the exact
glass composition required for 3D printing, thereby avoiding the mixing of
different batches or types of glass and reducing the risk of residual stresses
caused by mismatched thermal expansion. The rod tests additionally serve
as a baseline for comparing between the 3D-printed specimens with a
simpliﬁed geometry made from the same material.
The Vitri-Glass instrument comprises a heated chamber and a cruci-
ble, featuring concentric exit holes. Solid glass is initially input to the cru-
cible, and converted to glass rods as it passes through the heated chamber
into ambient conditions. In this study, soda-lime sheet glass “001100 Tekta
Clear” from Bullseye-Glass Co., was employed as the glass material. The
sheet glass was sourced as waste from a glass workshop (in the previously
unﬁred condition). Glass was loaded into the crucible, before heating the
chamber to 790 °C. A thermocouple was located at the chamber centre, to
continuously monitor temperature. Holding at 790 °C temperature for
60 min allows the glass to achieve an optimal viscosity for the “pulling”
process, with a calculated viscosity of 107.9 Poise. Subsequently, glass rods
with a diameter of 3.5 mm were drawn at a speed of 11.3 mm/s.
Positioned beneath the heated chamber is a motorised system,
responsible for pulling the glass from the crucible at constant speed. The
speed selected by the user dictates the diameter of the rod. Increasing the
pulling speed results in thinner glass rods and vice versa. The glass rods are
automatically cut to lengths of approximately one metre, with this process
repeating until the crucible is depleted. A single session was adequate to
produce enough glass rods required for all 15 of the 3D printed dog-bone
samples, with additional material remaining for glass rod testing.
To ensure a consistent diameter across all rods, regular inspections
using calipers were conducted. A general tolerance for the drawn glass rods
was 3.5 ± 0.1 mm. The diameters of the rods are listed in the supplementary
information (see Supplementary Table 1). One challenge in manufacturing
glass rods with this method is creating a homogeneous glass structure
without air bubbles. When sheet glass is used as the input material, air
bubbles can form between the sheets during heating. If the air has no escape
route,theywillbedrawnintotheglassrod,introducingweakpointsthatcan
affect the mechanical testing results. During the production of the 3.5 mm
rods the inclusion of air bubbles could be avoided.
3D-Printing process
The glass rods produced also served as the feedstock for the 3D printed dog-
bone samples. A Maple 4TM glass 3D printer (see Fig. 11) was employed to
additively manufacture the samples. The printer operates as a cartesian
FDM printer, with the nozzle manoeuvring along the XY-axis, while the
printing platform moves vertically in the Z-direction. The printer’s nozzle
washeated to940 °C,asmeasuredbythethermocouplepositioned inthe tip
of the nozzle. The nozzle extends into a heated chamber, maintained at
500 °C, slightly below the annealing temperature of the soda-lime silicate
glass. The printing platform is a ceramic plate, referred to as a kiln shelf.
Once at temperature, the glass rod can be extruded through the 1.5 mm
diameter outlet of the nozzle.
Each sample was printed individually to ensure precision and con-
sistency. A layer height and line width of 1 mm and 2 mm was selected,
respectively. Due to the nature of the process, the initial layer is compressed
into the platform to encourage bonding. This can result in the ﬁrst layer
measuringslightlyunder1 mm.Oncetheﬁrstlayeriscomplete,theprinting
platform moves down 1 mm, before depositing the next layer. The ﬁrst layer
speed was selected as 2 mm/s, while subsequent layers were printed at
4 mm/s.Uponconclusionoftheprintingprocess,eachsamplewasannealed
for 15 min at 500 °C, before passively cooling to room temperature in the
insulated chamber. The glass separates from the ceramic plate during
cooldown, as both materials contract at different rates. A release agent,
known as kiln wash, is applied before printing, to ensure safe and easy
separation. Although kiln wash residue adheres to the ﬁrst layer of the glass
specimens, it is not expected to inﬂuence the test results due to the chosen
testing orientation. Additionally, out of the experience of the authors there
will be no signiﬁcant residual stress because of the subsequent annealing
process. Residual stresses could not be monitored using optical devices, due
to the intransparency of the 3D printed specimen.

(excluding glass) using a dog-bone tensile test25, the brittleness of glass is
considered here and a bending test is performed instead (tensile strength =
bending tensile strength). Bending testing methods are typical for brittle
materials (EN 84319, EN 1288-318).
A novel aspect of this study is the use of dog-bone test geometry in a
bending setup, which is made possible by the design freedom of AM. The
selected test geometry including the dimensions can be found in Fig. 6. The
dog-bone geometry helps reduce stress concentrations at the support and
load points due to the increased surface area, thereby promoting failure in
the centre of the specimen’s narrow section (see Fig. 4). The geometry was
additionally validated using FEM analysis to ensure that no signiﬁcant shear
stresses occurred in the narrow section. A comparative value is necessary to
evaluate the collected results. To establish this, individual thin glass rods are
tested using the same four-point bending setup.
These bending tests offer a relatively straightforward and efﬁcient
method of mechanical evaluation, requiring minimal specimen preparation
and a simple experimental setup. Established standards, such as EN 1288-
318, are available to provide guidance and ensure consistency. However, it
must be noted that for bending tests, specimens must exhibit high
straightness and geometric uniformity, as deviations may compromise the
accuracy and reliability of the results. Furthermore, elevated stress levels are
often observed in bending tests at the sample edges, which may inﬂuence
failure behaviour.
The method presented here can serve as a basis for the development of
an established standard for determining the strength of additively manu-
facturedglassstructures.Inthefuture,thiswillenableobjectivecomparisons
between different AM methods for glass.

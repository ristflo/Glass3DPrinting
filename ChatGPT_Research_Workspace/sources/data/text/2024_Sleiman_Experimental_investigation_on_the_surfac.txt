1. Introduction
Silicate glasses play an important role in industrial use of 
glasses due to their optical, chemical, and thermal properties 
[1]. Fused silica in particular is distinguished by its low thermal
expansion coefficient and high thermal shock resistance [2]. It 
is used for a variety of optical components and optical fibers, 
but also finds application in chemical and medical apparatus 
engineering as well as in semiconductor technology [3]. 
However, the processing temperatures of over 2000 °C pose 
challenges in handling the low-viscosity fluid to form complex
or highly individualized components. 
Thanks to its freedom of design and process, the additive 
manufacturing of glass enables the realization of special and 
customized solutions with comparatively little effort compared 
to conventional manufacturing processes. Several approaches 
for the additive manufacturing of glass materials can be found 
in the literature. Xin et al. gave a comprehensive overview of 
the current state of research into additive glass processing 
methods in [4]. These can be broadly categorized into the 
following:
-
Sinter-based processes [5]
-
Stereolithography processes [6]
-
Extrusion processes [7]
-
Deposition welding processes [8][9]
In the investigations presented here, laser glass deposition 
(LGD), which is one of the deposition welding processes, is

Abstract
Laser Glass Deposition is an additive manufacturing method to produce individualized structural components out of glass. A CO2 laser is 
utilized as a heat source to melt fused silica filaments and transform them into a formable viscous state. The fiber filament is fed laterally under 
a defined angle into the process zone. The viscous filament is deposited layer-by-layer using a 3-axis linear system with an integrated rotational 
axis. To investigate the surface and volume quality of the additively manufactured fused silica components, fully dense test specimens are 
analyzed in this paper. Quality characteristics such as surface roughness, formation of boundary layers and optical transparency constitute the 
focus of the investigations. Consequently, fully dense glass components with homogeneous volume structures without pores and boundary 
layers and a surface roughness of less than 30 nm were printed successfully.

2. Methods
The experimental setup (Fig. 1) consists of a CO2 laser 
(Diamond J3, Coherent Inc.), a 4-axis system (Jenaer 
Antriebstechnik GmbH), a fiber feeding unit (MiniStirrer, 
HiTec Zang GmbH) to feed fused silica filament material 
(Weinert Optics GmbH) and the quartz glass substrate which 
serves as printing bed (Schröder Spezialglas GmbH). 
The radiation of the CO2 laser (PL = 200 W) is used as a heat 
source for melting and fusing the fused silica glass fiber 
filament (800 µm diameter) onto the quartz glass substrate. The 
laser beam diameter (dLaser) is defocused to form a beam 
diameter of 7 mm. The fiber is introduced laterally into the 
generated process zone at a rate of vFiber = 90 – 396 mm/min, 
and an angle of 55°. The glass melt can be deposited on the 
glass surface in any shape and contour using the 3 linear and a 
rotational axis. A camera (IDS Imaging Edmund Optics Ltd) is 
used for monitoring the deposition of the glass filament. The 
fiber feeding unit, the focus lens and the camera are attached to 
the Z-axis so that a new layer can be deposited under the same 
process conditions.
In the following investigations, cylindrical test specimens 
are printed to analyze the surface and volume quality. In order 
to print a dense glass volume, two concentric rings are printed 
with an overlap of 20% to each other. The inner ring is printed 
at a speed of vInside = 1 rpm and the outer ring at vOutside = 4 rpm. 
The printing path can be seen in principle in Fig 1 and is 
illustrated again in detail in Fig 2 (a).
3. Results 
3.1 Contour Study
To analyze the surface quality and investigate the influence of 
the test parameters, cylindrical test specimens were 
manufactured 
with 
different
fiber 
feed 
rates
vFiber = (90 – 396) mm/min. Exemplary images of the test 
specimens for the respective feed rates can be seen in Fig. 2 (b). 
An optically visible increase in surface quality can be observed 
in Fig. 2 (b) with a decreasing feed rate. For the highest value 
of vFiber = 396 mm/min, a wavy surface contour can be observed
along the complete structure. At a reduced feed rate, the 
individual layers can be fused together better, so that the wavy 
part of the surface shrinks. For vFiber = 180 mm/min the 
waviness was less prominent, while for vFiber = 126 mm/min a 
complete smooth area of 8 mm high was achieved. For vFiber =
90 mm/min the 10 mm high specimen was printed smoothly 
without any waviness. With the reduced feed rate, however, an 
increase in the flank angle can also be observed, which is 
graphical represented in Fig. 3. With the highest feed rate, a 
good approximation to the target contour can be observed, with 
a flank angle of 2.15°.
With the
lowest
value of 
vFiber = 90 mm/min a flank angle of 14.04° +/- 0.93° can be 
observed. The slower feed rate reduces the mass flow rate 
supplied, which leads to increased heat input at constant PL, 
causing the molten glass to spread further. As the laser spot
diameter is larger than the overlap zone, the layers slowly fall 
into each other as the number of layers increases. At high feed 
rates, this effect is not significant, as the high volume flow in 
the process zone reduces the heat input, resulting in a higher 
viscosity and a lower surface tension that influences the 
melting dynamics.
3.2 Surface Roughness Study
To analyze the surface quality of the additively 
manufactured test specimens, the surface roughness was 
determined exclusively on the smooth areas without boundary 
layers. It is important to note that the samples were not prepared 
by subsequent polishing but were measured directly from the 
printing process. The analysis was carried out using a tactile 
profile measuring device MarSurf LD 130 from Mahr GmbH. 
The measurements were carried out in accordance with the 
standards DIN EN ISO 1661021 and DIN EN ISO 4287 for 
surface roughness measurements to ensure comparability with 
industrially manufactured glass components. The roughness 
profile of an example measurement for vFiber = 90 mm/min can 
be seen in Fig. 2 (c). An arithmetic roughness of 21 nm was 
determined for a measuring distance of 1.26 mm. The 
corresponding arithmetic roughness of the remaining test 
specimens are shown graphically in Fig. 4. A single specimen 
is measured at six positions to calculate an average value. This 
is carried out on four further test specimens of the same process 
parameters to be able to represent a standard deviation in the 
form of the error bars in Fig 3.

3.3 Optical and Volume Quality Study
To analyze the optical quality and volume homogeneity of 
the
additive manufactured
specimens, a beam profile 
measurement was carried out. The setup of the beam profile 
measurement is shown in Fig. 4. A He-Ne laser (632 nm) is 
used as the beam source, whose radiation is magnified and 
collimated to a beam diameter of 2 mm by two focusing lenses. 
This beam is incident vertically on the underside of the clamped 
specimen so that the radiation is first propagated through the 
substrate and then through the specimen and finally detected in 
the beam camera. The beam camera consists of a CCD camera, 
which is controlled by a Raspberry Pi 4b and outputs the 
detected light as a false color live image. The upper side of the 
test specimen was remelted after the printing of the last layer 
by rotating the specimen under CO2 laser radiation to obtain a 
homogeneous surface. The entire specimen can be seen in 
Fig. 5 (b) and a view from above through the complete 
specimen can be seen in Fig. 5 (c) already suggesting a bubble-
free volume. 
Fig. 5 (a) shows the beam profile measurement at a distance 
of 30 mm from the focus. It can be observed that the beam 
profile has an elliptical shape and thus deviates from the 
circular input profile. The explanation is that due to the additive 
manufacturing process, no rotationally symmetrical sphere has 
formed on the surface, but the curvature is more pronounced on 
one side than on the other. What is remarkable, however, is the 
homogeneity of the beam profile, which shows neither bubbles 
nor boundary layer-related distortions for an additively 
manufactured component. This is a clear indication that the 
printed material has no boundary layers either in the transition 
area from the substrate to the printed material or in the internal 
layer transitions. The results show that targeted beam shaping 
is also possible with a suitable printing strategy for the surface 
contour.

4. Conclusion
Additive manufactured test specimens were produced using 
Laser Glass Deposition and analysed to investigate the surface 
and volume quality. The cylinder-shaped test specimens were 
printed by depositing two concentric rings with an overlap of 
20%. The printing parameters (PL = 200 W, vInside = 4 rpm, 
vOutside = 1 rpm, dL = 7 mm) were kept constant except for 
vFiber = (90 , 108, 126, 180, 396) mm/min. The resulting 
component contour deviated more strongly from the target 
contour as the feed rate decreased. The slower feed rate 
increased the heat input into the process zone, which led to 
increased flank formation on the outer contour. On the other 
hand, the increased heat input allowed the individual layers to 
fuse together completely, resulting in a smooth surface free of 
waves and boundary layers. The surface roughness of the 
smooth areas of each specimen was measured. The arithmetric
roughness of the specimens was measured at different points 
for several specimens along a measuring distance of 1.26 mm. 
The lowest arithmetric
roughness was determined for 
vFiber = 90 mm/min at 21 nm. Furthermore, the volume 
homogeneity and optical quality were demonstrated by a beam 
profile measurement. The transmitted beam showed a slight 
ellipticity, but was generally characterised by a homogeneous 
intensity distribution, which was free of bubble or volume 
boundary layer distortions.
The results show the strengths and challenges of the LGD 
method. It is possible to produce dense, smooth and boundary 
layer-free glass components with arithmetric roughness’s in the 
nanometer range. This opens up the possibility of extending the 
limits of modern glass production and realising components 
with new or integrated functions. The main challenge lies in 
contour accuracy and contour retention to form individulized 
optical functionalities, which will be focused on in future 
studys.
